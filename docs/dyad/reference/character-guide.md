## Candidate character rules and writing principles

1. **Make them inhabit the situation.** Accept the supplied fictional facts while preserving what is established versus merely alleged. A character's accusation, denial, or interpretation does not itself settle what happened; do not force truth, sincere belief, or an innocent explanation where the producer has left it open. Start with something one character wants, objects to, notices, or does. Avoid repeating the premise as exposition. Their exchanges must affect what happens next.

2. **Keep the target and alignment straight.** Know whether each line addresses the partner, an audience member, or an absent person. Carry forward agreement, disagreement, and new information. A shift in allegiance needs a recognizable reason.

3. **Give Shanny room to build a case when she is defending herself or complaining.** Begin with the immediate issue, then let her connect it to her effort, treatment, burden, or standing when relevant. Use direct address, clause chains, restarts, rhetorical questions, and selective repetition. A repeated point should add pressure, a new implication, or another grievance; avoid empty paraphrase.

4. **Keep Shanny’s courteous wording tied to its purpose.** Affectionate address may welcome someone, solicit agreement, dismiss them, or soften a refusal. In a refusal, the refusal must remain clear. In welcome or support, do not automatically turn kindness into a hidden attack. Practical help can coexist with irritation; neither providing help nor saying thanks determines the facial expression. A completed favor may stay completed without a new grievance.

5. **Treat a declared ending as something she says, until the scene actually ends.** A goodbye, rejection, or claim that she is finished may be followed by further complaint. Use that continuation when the exchange supports it. Do not make every goodbye reopen the argument.

6. **Let Shanny’s theatrical authority be situational.** Grand declarations, moral certainty, performative finality, and ceremonial dismissal can fit an attempt to establish standing or reclaim control. Carry the form into the fictional world when it fits. Do not make every ordinary disagreement become a spell, rant, or breakdown.

7. **Make Rev’s immediate replies specific.** In ordinary responses, agreement, or correction, he can pick up one detail, repeat or narrow a claim, give a compact explanation, or offer a practical response. Short affirmations and imperfect spoken phrasing are useful. A request can leave room for scarcity or refusal; receiving something he wants can prompt expansive praise and repeated thanks. Do not give every reply the polish of an essay.

8. **Let Rev take the floor when the situation warrants it.** Personal interests can prompt a ranked explanation, favorite example, autobiographical story, or enthusiastic comparison. Public accounts, defense of the pair, and distressed appeals can also become extended turns. He can explicitly hand the floor back after a tangent. “Rev always speaks briefly” is not a rule.

9. **Do not make Rev a permanent straight man.** He can support, amplify, object, correct, appeal, joke, or withdraw. His correction need not resolve the dispute, and his agreement need not be wise. Select his response from the immediate exchange.

10. **Use the pair’s mismatch to create movement.** A narrow correction can meet a broadened grievance; an explanation can be interrupted by a personal tangent; one partner’s concern can meet simple confirmation. During shared interests, Rev can press for a specific answer while Shanny supplies more examples or maintains a broad preference. A correction may be accepted, qualified, or left unresolved while the shared discussion continues. Show the next response instead of treating disagreement itself as a trigger for escalation.

11. **Let them contribute to a shared idea.** One can notice a connection; the other can add an example, implication, memory, or playful extension. Mutual reinforcement can make an ordinary observation feel personally significant. They can correct a detail while keeping the same overall position. When new information makes an outsider a shared target, use that information to explain the alignment change. Shared interests and affection also sustain cooperation; a common enemy is not required.

12. **Keep the language conversational and the signature moments earned.** Allow fragments, repeated starts, ordinary replies, and stretches without punchlines. Use a characteristic laugh, murmur, elongated close, or burst of complaint when the situation supplies a reason. Rare memorable behavior should remain rare enough to retain its character.

## Boundaries that keep the characters useful

- These are rules for generating recognizable fiction, with conditional habits rather than universal claims about real people.
- Keep source allegations, diagnoses, disputed events, and private biographies out of new fictional canon unless the producer explicitly establishes an adapted story fact.
- When adapting source lore into a new fictional premise, preserve a useful relationship move while changing the world, object, stakes, and wording. An audience-access dispute can become a dispute over a ship's visitor pass; it need not reproduce the original incident. This does not authorize replacing the producer's current premise.
- Escalation needs an exchange that sustains it. Agreement, a practical answer, partial repair, distraction, or an unresolved ordinary ending are available outcomes.
- Do not force equal line lengths, one joke per line, a fixed sequence of moods, or every known habit into each scene.
